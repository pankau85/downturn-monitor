Every indicator is converted to a continuous score from 1 to 5 using five thresholds: the values at which it would read exactly 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, with straight-line interpolation in between. The thresholds come from the published literature on each indicator (the Sahm rule’s 0.50 pp, the Chicago Fed’s −0.70, the conventional 10% correction and 20% bear market) and from how each series behaved around past recessions. A few indicators use a richer rule: the yield curve is cycle-aware (the danger window is the re-steepening after an inversion), credit spreads take the higher of a level score and a momentum score, CPI is two-sided, and consumer sentiment and card delinquencies blend level and change. Individual indicators are labelled on equal bands (Benign below 1.8, Guarded to 2.6, Elevated to 3.4, High to 4.2, Severe above).

Indicators are averaged within each pillar using the weights shown, and pillars are averaged into the composite. Averaging thirty scores compresses the range, so the composite, the two lenses and the pillars use the calibrated bands above: on 1990–2026 history every recession reached High or Severe, while the market-stress episodes that did not become recessions topped out at Elevated. The gauge gives each band an equal arc and marks the hand-over values. Indicators with no data drop out and the remaining weights are rescaled; the coverage figure tells you how much of the intended weight is in play. The Economy lens averages the rates, labor, growth and consumer pillars; the Markets lens averages credit conditions and market stress.

The composite is a prior, not a forecast. No single indicator has a clean record, and the set as a whole has read Elevated in several episodes that ended without a recession (see the track record above). Its job is to tell you how much of the usual pre-downturn pattern is present, and which pieces are driving it.

Known limitations

  • The Conference Board LEI and consumer confidence are proprietary and not included; the OECD and Philadelphia Fed leading indexes are candidates for a later version.
  • ISM’s PMI has no free machine-readable source and is not included; the regional Fed surveys cover the same ground.
  • FRED carries only the trailing three years of the ICE BofA high-yield and investment-grade indexes, so the Baa corporate spread supplies the long credit history. S&P 500 and MOVE history come from Yahoo Finance; if that feed fails, equity indicators fall back to FRED’s ten years of S&P 500 data.
  • Historical readings use today’s revised data (except the real-time Sahm rule) and assumed publication lags, so the back-test is cleaner than a true real-time test would be.
  • Market data refreshes each morning for the prior close; monthly releases appear after the second daily run on release days.
